DOCUMENT TYPE: Knowledge Base Reference
TOPIC: ESOP, RSU, and Equity Compensation in Indian Employment Contracts
APPLICABLE TO: offer_letter, employment_contract

---

OVERVIEW

Equity compensation — ESOPs (Employee Stock Option Plans) and RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) — is increasingly common in Indian startups, unicorns, and listed product companies. Understanding your equity grant is critical because it can represent a significant portion of your total compensation.

India has a specific legal framework for ESOPs under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI regulations.

---

TYPES OF EQUITY COMPENSATION

1. ESOP (Employee Stock Option Plan)
   An ESOP gives you the RIGHT (option) to purchase company shares at a predetermined price (exercise price or strike price) in the future, subject to vesting.
   You are not given shares immediately — you earn the right to buy them over time.

2. RSU (Restricted Stock Unit)
   An RSU is a PROMISE to give you actual shares after a vesting period.
   You do not pay an exercise price — the shares are granted to you (subject to tax).
   More common in listed companies and Indian subsidiaries of US-headquartered companies.

3. SAR (Stock Appreciation Rights)
   Less common in India. Pays you the difference between the strike price and the fair market value at exercise, in cash. No share ownership.

4. ESPP (Employee Stock Purchase Plan)
   Allows employees to purchase company shares at a discount (typically 5–15%) from market price through payroll deductions.
   Common in US-listed company subsidiaries operating in India.

---

VESTING SCHEDULE — WHAT IS STANDARD

Vesting = the schedule over which your equity becomes yours.

Standard vesting in Indian startups and tech companies:
- Total vesting period: 4 years
- Cliff: 1 year (you earn nothing in the first 12 months; at month 13 you receive 25% of your total grant)
- Monthly/quarterly vesting after cliff: Remaining 75% vests in equal monthly or quarterly tranches over 36 months.

This is the "4-year vest, 1-year cliff" structure — the global standard.

Red flag: 5+ year vesting period — reduces the likelihood of any real payout.
Red flag: No cliff mentioned — may indicate vesting from day one, which can also mean the grant is smaller.
Red flag: 6-month or 2-year cliff — non-standard.

ESOP vesting example:
- Grant: 10,000 options at ₹10/share exercise price
- Year 1 cliff: 2,500 options vest
- Months 13–48: ~208 options vest per month
- At the end of 4 years: All 10,000 options are vested.

---

EXERCISE PRICE / STRIKE PRICE

The price at which you can buy the shares when you exercise your options.

Standard in Indian startups: Set at Fair Market Value (FMV) at the time of grant. For private companies, FMV is determined by a registered merchant banker.

Low exercise price (e.g., face value of ₹10 for a ₹1,000 FMV share): Very favorable to employee. Common in early-stage grants.

High exercise price: If the company's valuation has declined since your grant date, your options may be "underwater" (exercise price > current FMV). They would have zero economic value.

Red flag: Exercise price not defined in the offer letter — you won't know the cost of exercising.
Red flag: Exercise price subject to change at company's discretion.

---

EXERCISE WINDOW

The window is the period within which you can exercise your vested options.

Standard in Indian startups: 90 days after leaving the company (for good leavers).
US-standard best practice: 5–10 years from grant date.

Red flag: 30-day exercise window — extreme pressure to make a large financial decision quickly.
Red flag: Options expire immediately on resignation — no exercise window at all. This is extremely unfair.
Red flag: Exercise window not defined in the contract — will default to ESOP policy document.

"Good Leaver" vs "Bad Leaver" distinction:
- Good leaver (resigned, made redundant): Receives full vested options with standard exercise window.
- Bad leaver (terminated for cause): May forfeit all options, including vested ones. This is legally permitted in India.

Red flag: Definition of "bad leaver" is so broad it includes voluntary resignation — employee forfeits vested options just by resigning.

---

TAXATION OF ESOPs IN INDIA

ESOPs are taxed at TWO points in India:

POINT 1 — AT EXERCISE (purchasing the shares):
Income Tax applies as perquisite income = (FMV on exercise date − exercise price) × number of options exercised.
Taxed as salary income at your applicable income tax slab rate.
Employer is required to deduct TDS on this perquisite.

POINT 2 — AT SALE (when you sell the shares):
Capital Gains Tax applies = (Sale price − FMV at exercise).
If held for < 2 years (unlisted shares) or < 1 year (listed shares): Short-term capital gains (STCG) at applicable income tax slab.
If held for ≥ 2 years (unlisted) or ≥ 1 year (listed): Long-term capital gains (LTCG) — 20% with indexation (unlisted) or 10% above ₹1 lakh (listed, Section 112A).

Budget 2024 changes: LTCG on listed securities now taxed at 12.5% (up from 10%), effective July 23, 2024.

Special ESOP deferral for startups (DPIIT-recognized):
- As per Finance Act 2020, ESOP tax at exercise is DEFERRED by 5 years for eligible DPIIT-recognized startups.
- This means you don't pay tax immediately on exercise — you can wait until you sell the shares.
- Significant benefit for startups with long lock-ins before IPO or acquisition.

Red flag: Contract or ESOP policy doesn't mention DPIIT startup deferral eligibility — employees may be missing this benefit.

---

ESOP IN PRIVATE VS LISTED COMPANIES

Private companies:
- Options can only be exercised if there is a liquidity event — IPO, acquisition, secondary sale.
- A large ESOP grant at a startup is only valuable IF the company achieves a successful exit.
- Example: 10,000 options at ₹10 strike price; company sells at ₹200/share FMV → profit = ₹190 × 10,000 = ₹19,00,000 before tax.
- Illiquid until a liquidity event. May become worthless if startup fails.

Listed companies (NSE/BSE):
- RSUs typically vest and are immediately sellable (subject to insider trading lock-up periods for insiders).
- More liquid but subject to public market volatility.

---

ESOP POLICY DOCUMENT — SEPARATE FROM CONTRACT

Many Indian startups give you a summary of the ESOP grant in the offer letter but the detailed terms are in a separate ESOP Policy or ESOP Trust document.

Always request and read the full ESOP Policy before signing. Key things to check:
1. Vesting schedule (cliff, monthly/quarterly).
2. Accelerated vesting clause — do all options vest immediately if the company is acquired?
3. Exercise window after leaving.
4. Good leaver / bad leaver definitions.
5. Anti-dilution protection (or lack of it).
6. Any right of first refusal by the company if you try to sell.
7. Lock-up period after IPO.

---

DILUTION AND ANTI-DILUTION

Dilution: When the company raises new funding rounds, existing shareholders (including ESOP holders) own a smaller percentage of the company. Their absolute number of shares stays the same, but the total pie grows.

Example: You own 10,000 shares out of 1,00,00,000 total (0.01%). After a new round issues 50,00,000 new shares, you own 10,000 out of 1,50,00,000 (0.0067%) — diluted.

Anti-dilution protection: Provisions in the ESOP or shareholder agreement that protect option holders from excessive dilution. Rarely given to employees in early rounds.

Red flag: No information on current cap table or total diluted share count — you cannot evaluate the real value of your grant without knowing your percentage ownership.

---

WHAT TO ASK ABOUT YOUR ESOP GRANT

1. How many options are being granted? What is the current FMV per share?
2. What is the exercise/strike price?
3. What is the vesting schedule (cliff period, monthly/quarterly after cliff)?
4. What is the exercise window after I leave the company?
5. Is this company DPIIT-recognized? Does the 5-year ESOP tax deferral apply?
6. What is the total fully-diluted share count? What percentage of the company does my grant represent?
7. Is there accelerated vesting on acquisition or change of control?
8. Is there a right of first refusal preventing me from selling on secondary markets?
9. Can you share the full ESOP Policy document?
10. What is the last valuation / FMV per share? When was it determined?
