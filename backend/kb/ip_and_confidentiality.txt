DOCUMENT TYPE: Knowledge Base Reference
TOPIC: IP Assignment, Confidentiality, and Intellectual Property Clauses
APPLICABLE TO: employment_contract, offer_letter, nda

---

OVERVIEW

Intellectual Property (IP) assignment clauses are among the most far-reaching provisions in any employment contract. They determine who owns the work you produce — including code, designs, inventions, research, and creative works — during and sometimes after your employment.

Confidentiality clauses (also called NDAs embedded in employment contracts) govern what company information you can retain and use.

These clauses are generally enforceable in India, especially when the work was produced within the scope of employment using company resources.

---

LEGAL FRAMEWORK

Relevant laws:
- The Patents Act, 1970: Section 6 — inventions made by an employee using employer's resources and in the course of their employment belong to the employer.
- The Copyright Act, 1957: Section 17 — works made in the course of employment vest in the employer automatically, unless there is a specific agreement otherwise.
- The Indian Contract Act, 1872: Governs confidentiality agreements — enforceable during and post-employment if reasonable in scope.
- Information Technology Act, 2000: Section 43A and Section 72A — data breach liability; relevant for companies handling personal data.
- Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act): India's new data protection law — employers that handle employee and client personal data must comply; employees handling sensitive data have obligations under this new law.

Key principle: Work done during employment hours, using company equipment or resources, on topics within your job scope belongs to your employer even without an explicit IP clause. The IP clause formalizes and often expands this to include work done on personal time if it is "related to the company's business."

---

IP ASSIGNMENT — WHAT IS STANDARD

Standard IP assignment clause:
"All inventions, discoveries, developments, improvements, and works of authorship made or conceived by the employee during their employment, in connection with the business of the company or using the company's resources, shall be the exclusive property of the company."

This is reasonable if limited to:
(a) Work done during employment hours.
(b) Work done on company equipment.
(c) Work related to the company's actual business.

Standard scope: Any code, software, invention, process, design, or creative work related to your job role and done in the course of employment.

Standard duration: During employment (automatic assignment upon creation).

---

IP ASSIGNMENT — RED FLAGS

Red flag 1 — Perpetual, unlimited scope:
"All inventions, ideas, or works conceived or developed at any time, whether or not related to the company's business, are assigned to the company."

→ This clause attempts to capture your personal projects, open-source contributions, and side work completely unrelated to the company's business. Courts would likely read this down, but it's a negotiation point.

Red flag 2 — Prior inventions not carved out:
If you have existing personal projects, open-source repositories, or inventions before joining, the contract should explicitly carve these out.

Best practice: Attach a schedule of prior inventions to the offer letter or appointment letter before signing, listing everything you want to retain ownership of.

Red flag 3 — Moral rights waived:
"The employee irrevocably waives all moral rights in the works assigned."
→ Under Indian law (Copyright Act, 1957, Section 57), moral rights (right to attribution, right against derogatory treatment) cannot be fully waived — this clause may not be fully enforceable, but it signals an aggressive legal posture.

Red flag 4 — Post-employment assignment:
"The employee assigns all future inventions related to the company's work for a period of 2 years after leaving."
→ Extremely aggressive. Indian courts are unlikely to enforce assignment of work done after employment ends unless the employee used confidential information directly.

Red flag 5 — Invention notification required:
"Employee must notify the company of any invention, whether or not related to the company, within 7 days of conception."
→ Highly aggressive — requires disclosing all personal invention ideas to the employer.

---

CARVE-OUT FOR PERSONAL PROJECTS

Best practice before signing: Compile a list of all personal projects (GitHub repos, apps, patents, papers) you are currently working on or have created. Present this list to the employer in writing and request a clause:

"The assignment obligations in this agreement shall not apply to prior inventions listed in Exhibit A attached hereto, nor to inventions developed entirely on the employee's own time, using no company resources, and not related to the company's current or reasonably anticipated business."

Some progressive tech companies (particularly product companies in the US-India corridor) already include this carve-out by default. Many Indian IT services companies do not.

---

CONFIDENTIALITY CLAUSES — WHAT IS STANDARD

Standard confidentiality obligations:
- Protect company's trade secrets, client lists, pricing strategies, product roadmaps, and proprietary technology.
- Duration: During employment AND for a reasonable period post-employment (typically 2–5 years for truly confidential trade secrets; can be indefinite for information that remains confidential).
- Scope: Limited to information that is genuinely confidential — not general industry knowledge or skills acquired through experience.

Standard carve-outs (information that cannot be kept confidential):
(a) Information already public knowledge.
(b) Information the employee knew before joining.
(c) Information received from a third party without confidentiality obligation.
(d) Information required to be disclosed by law or court order.

---

CONFIDENTIALITY — RED FLAGS

Red flag: "All information about the company" is confidential — so broad it could include the company's name, industry, and public announcements.

Red flag: "Employee shall not disclose any information about their employment, salary, or role" — this is an attempt to prevent salary transparency, which is a right employees should be cautious about waiving. Many jurisdictions recognize salary discussion as protected concerted activity.

Red flag: Confidentiality obligation survives indefinitely with no carve-out for publicly available information — overly broad.

Red flag: Penalty clause for breach of confidentiality without defining what constitutes a breach — leaves employee exposed to arbitrary claims.

---

NON-DISCLOSURE OF SALARY (CONFIDENTIALITY OF COMPENSATION)

Many Indian employment contracts include a clause prohibiting employees from discussing their salary with colleagues.

Legal status: Not illegal under Indian law, but ethically questionable and often unenforceable in practice.

Practical impact: If all employees stay silent about salaries, pay inequality persists. Discussing salary with a trusted colleague is generally protected by labor norms, even if technically a contractual violation.

Red flag: Penalties for "salary disclosure" — disproportionate to any real harm.

---

DATA PROTECTION AND DPDP ACT (2023)

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is India's first comprehensive data protection law, passed in August 2023. Key implications for employees:

1. If your job involves handling customer, employee, or partner personal data, you have legal obligations as a "data fiduciary."
2. Breaching data security protocols can now carry regulatory penalties.
3. Employment contracts in data-sensitive roles (product, engineering, customer support) increasingly include specific data protection obligations.
4. Red flag: Contract says employee bears personal liability for data breaches — in most cases, liability rests with the company as the data fiduciary, not the individual employee.

---

SOURCE CODE AND OPEN-SOURCE CONTRIBUTIONS

If your role involves software development:

Standard: Company owns all code written for the company's products or services during employment.
Complex area: Open-source contributions.

If the company's products use open-source code (most do), and if you contribute to upstream open-source projects:
- Contribution to a public open-source project during working hours → likely company property if related to your work.
- Contribution to a completely unrelated open-source project on personal time → likely yours.
- Red flag: Contract says all GitHub contributions, including to personal repos, belong to the company.

Best practice: Get explicit written permission from the employer before making open-source contributions on company time, even to unrelated projects.

---

KEY QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE SIGNING

1. Does the IP assignment clause cover work done entirely on personal time with personal equipment?
2. Can I carve out my prior inventions and personal projects? Will the company acknowledge this in writing?
3. What information is defined as confidential? Is there a carve-out for general skills and knowledge?
4. Can I contribute to open-source projects? Does the company require disclosure or approval?
5. What are the post-employment confidentiality obligations and for how long?
6. Does the contract restrict salary disclosure to colleagues?
