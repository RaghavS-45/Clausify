OVERVIEW

Negotiation is one of the highest-ROI activities you can engage in during a job search. A ₹1 lakh increase in base salary typically compounds across salary revisions, bonuses, and future job offers. Yet most Indian job seekers accept the first number they are offered.

This KB covers negotiation strategies, timing, scripts, and specific tactics for Indian employment contexts in 2024.

---

WHY YOU SHOULD ALWAYS NEGOTIATE

Market research (LinkedIn Salary Insights 2023, Naukri Salary Trends 2024):
- Approximately 70–80% of employers in India leave room in the initial offer for negotiation.
- Candidates who negotiate receive offers that are on average 5–15% higher.
- Fewer than 40% of Indian job seekers attempt to negotiate their compensation.

The cost of not negotiating is permanent. If you accept ₹10 LPA when you could have negotiated to ₹11 LPA:
- Year 1 loss: ₹1,00,000
- Over 5 years (with similar % increases): ₹5–7 lakh cumulative loss.

---

WHEN TO NEGOTIATE

BEST TIME: After receiving a formal written offer, before signing it.
Do NOT negotiate: During the first interview round. It signals that you are focused on money before demonstrating value.
DO negotiate: After the final interview, when the company has signaled strong interest.

You have maximum leverage when the company has already decided they want you. Once you sign the offer letter, leverage drops to near zero.

---

THE INDIA-SPECIFIC SALARY DISCLOSURE PROBLEM

Many Indian employers ask for your "current CTC" before making an offer. This is problematic:
- It anchors your new offer to your old salary, not to the market rate for the role.
- It perpetuates salary inequality, especially for candidates who were underpaid in previous roles.

Legal context: Several Indian states and cities are discussing pay transparency legislation (following US states like California, New York). As of 2024, India has no nationwide ban on asking for previous salary. However:
- Karnataka: Draft IT policy proposes restrictions on using previous salary as a basis for offers.
- Several large tech companies (Google, Microsoft, Amazon India) have voluntarily stopped asking for current CTC.

Strategy: When asked for current CTC, shift the conversation to expected CTC. You can say:
"I'm looking for a compensation package that reflects the market rate for this role. Based on my research, I'm targeting ₹[X]–₹[Y] for this position. I'm happy to share more context once we've discussed the role's scope."

If forced to disclose: Quote your total CTC (including variable, PF, ESOP value), not just your base salary. This is accurate and maximizes your anchor.

---

ANCHORING — THE MOST POWERFUL NEGOTIATION TACTIC

Anchoring is the practice of stating a number first to influence the range of counteroffers.

If you give a number first: Give it high — at or above your target. The final agreed number is almost always lower than your anchor.

Example:
You want ₹12 LPA. The market range is ₹10–14 LPA.
Bad anchor: "I was hoping for around ₹12 LPA." → Company counters with ₹11 LPA.
Good anchor: "Based on my research and the responsibilities of this role, I'm targeting ₹13.5–14 LPA." → Company counters with ₹12–13 LPA. Better outcome.

When not to anchor first: If you genuinely don't know the market rate, let the company make the first offer. Ask: "Can you share the budgeted range for this role?"

---

WHAT TO NEGOTIATE (BEYOND BASE SALARY)

Base salary is the most important — it compounds. But you can also negotiate:

1. Joining bonus: Particularly useful if you are forgoing unvested bonus/ESOP from your previous employer. Ask to be made "whole."

2. Variable pay targets and measurement: Ask for the targets in writing and negotiate for 100% payout criteria that are realistic.

3. Role grade / level: Moving up a level (e.g., SDE-2 instead of SDE-1) can be worth ₹5–20 LPA over the next 2–3 years at the same company.

4. ESOP grant size: Often negotiable, especially at early-stage startups. Ask: "Is there flexibility in the number of options in my grant?"

5. Notice period: Negotiate it down before joining — far easier than after.

6. Probation period: Ask for a shorter probation or immediate confirmation.

7. Work location / WFH arrangement: Negotiate in writing, not just verbally.

8. Relocation allowance: If moving cities, negotiate the amount and scope.

9. Learning budget: "Can you include a ₹50,000 annual L&D budget in my offer?"

10. Health insurance: Family coverage, parental coverage, higher sum insured.

---

BATNA (BEST ALTERNATIVE TO A NEGOTIATED AGREEMENT)

Your negotiating power comes from your alternatives.

Strong BATNA: Another competing offer. This is the single most powerful negotiating tool.
When you have a competing offer: "I have another offer at ₹X. I prefer this company because [specific reasons]. Can you match or come close to that?"
Companies typically respond to competing offers. It removes the emotion and makes it a market question.

Weak BATNA: Unemployment. If this is your only offer, your leverage is limited. Focus on non-monetary items (role clarity, WFH, learning budget) where the company's cost to accommodate is low.

---

HOW TO HANDLE THE "THIS IS OUR BEST OFFER" PUSHBACK

"I understand this is your best offer on base salary. Could we explore alternatives — perhaps a higher variable target, an additional joining bonus, or a larger ESOP grant that gets me to my target total compensation?"

Breaking the package into components often unlocks flexibility even when a single number is "fixed."

---

OFFER LETTER CLAUSE NEGOTIATION

Beyond compensation, these contract terms are negotiable and worth the conversation:

1. Notice period: "I'd like to request that the notice period be reduced from 90 to 60 days, which is the market standard for my level. Would that work?"

2. Employment bond: "I noticed there's a ₹1,00,000 bond for 2 years. Could we add a pro-rata reduction clause, so the amount decreases proportionally for each month I serve?"

3. Non-compete scope: "The non-compete clause appears quite broad. Could it be limited to direct competitors in [specific segment] rather than the entire technology industry?"

4. IP carve-out: "I have a few personal projects I've been working on prior to joining. Could we add an exhibit listing these as prior inventions excluded from the IP assignment clause?"

5. Probation terms: "Could we specify that the probation period will not be extended beyond [date] unless there is a documented performance issue?"

NEGOTIATION DO's AND DON'Ts IN INDIA

DO:
✓ Get everything negotiated agreed in writing — either updated offer letter or a written email confirmation.
✓ Be specific: "I'm asking for ₹12.5 LPA" — not "Can you do better?"
✓ Express enthusiasm for the role alongside the negotiation. "I'm excited to join — I just want to make sure we're aligned on terms."
✓ Give the company time to respond — don't ask for an answer in 5 minutes.
✓ Know the market rate (Glassdoor India, Levels.fyi, Naukri salary reports, AmbitionBox) before negotiating.

DON'T:
✗ Lie about competing offers — companies sometimes verify. If you imply a competing offer, be ready to walk away if they don't match.
✗ Apologize for negotiating. "Sorry to ask, but..." makes you seem uncertain.
✗ Accept verbally and then try to renegotiate — signals bad faith.
✗ Negotiate multiple times in succession — make your best ask once, clearly.
✗ Disclose your maximum willingness to pay — "I can't go below ₹X" anchors the company at X.

SALARY NEGOTIATION SCRIPTS (FOR INDIAN CONTEXT)

SCRIPT 1 — Asking for a higher offer after receiving one:
"Thank you for the offer. I'm genuinely excited about the role and the team. I was hoping we could discuss the compensation — based on my research and the scope of the role, I was targeting something in the range of ₹[X]–₹[Y]. Is there flexibility to get closer to that range?"

SCRIPT 2 — Using a competing offer:
"I have a competing offer at ₹[X] in hand. I prefer your company because [specific reason]. I wanted to give you the opportunity to match before I make my decision. Can we get to ₹[Y]?"

SCRIPT 3 — Asking for non-monetary items:
"I understand the base salary is fixed at ₹[X]. Given that, could we look at a ₹[Y] joining bonus to compensate for the variable bonus I'll be forgoing at my current company? Alternatively, could we discuss an enhanced L&D budget or a larger ESOP grant?"

SCRIPT 4 — Negotiating contract terms (notice period):
"I noticed the notice period is 90 days. I'd like to request this be reduced to 60 days, which is more standard for my level in the market. Is that something we can adjust in the appointment letter?"

INDUSTRY-SPECIFIC NEGOTIATION NOTES

IT SERVICES (TCS, INFOSYS, WIPRO):
- Limited flexibility on salary for mass-hire programs — offers are standardized.
- More flexibility exists at the lateral hire level (3+ years experience).
- Joining bonus is rarely offered but can be requested for candidates with competing offers.
- Bond and notice period are harder to negotiate — company policy-driven.

PRODUCT / STARTUPS:
- Most flexibility. Salary, ESOP grant size, joining bonus, WFH, and level are all negotiable.
- Use market data (Levels.fyi for product companies, AmbitionBox, Glassdoor India).
- Competing offers carry significant weight.

BFSI (BANKING, FINANCIAL SERVICES):
- CTC structure often includes a large variable — negotiate the payout criteria.
- Notice period is typically 60–90 days; hard to reduce.
- ESOP/RSU less common; joining bonus for specialized roles is negotiable.

CONSULTING (BIG 4):
- Grade/band is often non-negotiable; compensation follows band.
- Offer for a higher grade/band is the key negotiation.
- Joining bonus for laterals with competing offers is sometimes available.

STARTUPS (PRE-SERIES B):
- ESOP grant is the primary negotiation lever.
- Ask about: company valuation, total share count, expected liquidity timeline.
- Salary may be below market; ESOP is the upside — ensure you understand both clearly.
